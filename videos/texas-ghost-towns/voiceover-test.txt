Texas is loud. Big cities, oil money, ranch land running past the horizon.
But drive far enough off the interstate and the noise stops.
You start finding places where thousands of people built whole lives.
Then they packed up and left every last brick behind.
These are twelve of them. Most people have never heard of a single one.
Number one was a city of five thousand people.
Texas erased it from the map twice in eleven years.
Now it lies underwater in the bay where it stood. Let's count down.
Number twelve. Toyah, out in Reeves County, far west of the state.
Drive the back roads here and one thing stops you cold.
An enormous abandoned high school, alone in an empty field.
Shattered windows. Half-collapsed roof. It looks like a horror set.
Toyah started in the 1880s as a small trading post.
The name means flowing water, for the springs that fed the land.
In 1881 the Texas and Pacific Railroad came through, and Toyah boomed.
By 1914 it held over a thousand people.
Four churches. Two banks. Two hotels. A drugstore.
Then the railroad moved its operations away.
Even a small oil boom in the 1920s could not hold the town.
By the Great Depression the population had been cut in half.
It never recovered. Today maybe ninety people remain.
And that giant school still stands in the silence, where the trains forgot it.
A town killed by a railroad leaving. Number eleven died when one arrived.
Number eleven. Sherwood, in Irion County.
One beautiful stone courthouse from 1901 still stands here.
It sits in a town that basically does not exist anymore.
Sherwood was laid out in 1886.
When Irion County organized in 1889, this became the county seat.
By 1900 it was the only real town in the county.
Then the railroad came, and here is the pattern you will see all day.
In 1910 the line crossed the county and missed Sherwood completely.
A brand new rail town, Mertzon, sprang up right on the tracks.
Business loaded onto wagons and followed the trains.
In 1939 even the county government packed up and left for Mertzon.
Sherwood kept its grand, empty courthouse and almost nothing else.
About seventy people live there now.
The most handsome building in the county, in a town the railroad walked around.
Being bypassed is a slow death. Number ten is rarer. Someone bought it.
Number ten. Lobo, in Culberson County, with a twist nobody expects.
This whole abandoned West Texas town was bought by three men from Germany.
Lobo was founded in the 1880s.
In 1882 it became a water stop and depot on the Southern Pacific line.
It grew into a real community of more than a hundred people, ringed by farms.
The fall began in the 1950s when the railroad was abandoned.
The farms dried out around it.
By 1991 Lobo was completely deserted. A true ghost town.
Then a German tourist drove past a town for sale sign.
On November 5th, 2001, three men from Frankfurt bought the whole town.
Ten acres of Texas ghost town for about twenty thousand dollars.
Their plan was to fix the ruins and hold desert arts and music festivals.
Years later it was listed again, this time near a hundred thousand dollars.
A German owned American ghost town, still empty under the West Texas sky.
Number nine once gave Texas a university and a living legend.
Number nine. Independence, in Washington County, near Brenham.
Those lonely columns are all that is left of the original Baylor University.
The man who helped pay for it was Sam Houston himself.
Independence was founded in 1835.
By 1845 people called it the wealthiest community in Texas.
That year Baylor University was chartered and built its first campus here.
Sam Houston gave one of the first big donations to start the school.
He lived here in the 1850s, and in 1854 he was baptized in the Brazos River.
So how does a town this important turn into a ghost?
Same villain again. The railroads went somewhere else.
Without a line, Independence could not compete.
In 1885 Baylor gave up and moved to Waco, where it still sits today.
The university left. The people followed it out.
Now those stone columns stand in a quiet park, side by side with the silence.
Number eight was not killed by a missing railroad. It was killed by a beetle.
Number eight. Calvert, in Robertson County.
At its peak this was the fourth largest city in all of Texas.
Around ten thousand people. An opera house. Saloons and gambling halls.
In 1871 it claimed the largest cotton gin in the world.
Calvert was born in 1868 when the Houston and Texas Central Railway arrived.
Cotton was king, and Calvert was its court.
Merchants and workers poured in from everywhere.
So what brought the fourth largest city in Texas down? A bug.
The boll weevil devours cotton, and it swept through the fields.
Cotton prices collapsed at the same time, and the economy gutted out.
By 1900 the population had already fallen to about three thousand.
In 1938 the great cotton gin and oil press shut down for good.
Here is the eerie part. Calvert never tore itself down.
Its historic district holds dozens of full blocks, still standing, mostly empty.
A fourth largest city, frozen in time, knocked flat by an insect.
Number seven traded cotton for something shinier and far more deadly.
Number seven. Shafter, deep in Presidio County.
They called it the Silver Capital of Texas.
In under sixty years this district pulled nearly thirty-one million ounces of silver.
It started around 1880, when John Spencer found silver in the Chinati Mountains.
He partnered with a former colonel, William Shafter.
In 1883 they founded the Presidio Mining Company, and the town took the colonel's name.
At its height around 1940, Shafter held as many as four thousand people.
Company housing. Company store. Company doctor.
Your whole life owned by the mine.
But silver mining lives and dies on price.
When silver crashed in the 1930s, operations stalled.
Rising costs, a miner shortage, and a union fight finished it in the 1940s.
When nearby military fields closed after World War Two, the decline became a free fall.
From four thousand people down to a couple dozen.
The Silver Capital of Texas, handed back to the desert that hid the silver.
Number six died in a way you have driven past without ever noticing.
Number six. Glenrio, sitting right on the Texas and New Mexico line.
This is one of the best preserved ghost towns from the golden age of Route 66.
Frozen at the exact moment the road died.
Glenrio appeared around 1903 when the railroad came through.
Its real life began in 1926, when Route 66 was routed straight through town.
Suddenly Glenrio was a highway oasis.
Motels, gas stations, the Texas Longhorn Café, all fed by endless travelers.
The border made it strange. The Texas side had no bars, the county was dry.
The New Mexico side had no gas, the fuel tax was higher.
The post office sat on one side, the mail came from the other.
Then came the killer of a thousand small towns. The interstate.
By 1973 Interstate 40 was finished, and it bypassed Glenrio completely.
The travelers vanished almost overnight.
The motel, the café, the Texaco, the old roadbed, they all still stand.
A perfect snapshot of the day the Mother Road went quiet.
Number five was killed on purpose, by one grieving, furious man.
Number five. Helena, in Karnes County, once called the toughest town on earth.
This town did not just fade. The legend locals tell is darker.
They say one furious rancher destroyed it on purpose.
Helena was founded in 1852 and became the first seat of Karnes County.
A rough stop between San Antonio and the coast, with a violent name.
How violent? It gave its name to the Helena Duel.
Two men, left hands tied together, each given a short three-inch blade.
Here is the story that made Helena a ghost.
In 1884 the son of a wealthy rancher named William Butler was killed in town.
As the legend goes, Butler swore to kill the town that killed his son.
And he had the power to do it.
He offered the railroad free right-of-way, on one condition.
Route the tracks away from Helena, through a new town. They took the deal.
That detour, still called the Big Curve, strangled Helena.
Business followed the trains. In 1894 the county seat moved to Karnes City.
The toughest town on earth died slowly, they say, exactly as the father intended.
Number four was the largest company town in Texas. Owned, every brick.
Number four. Thurber, in Erath County, the largest company town in the state.
Ten thousand people lived here, and not one of them owned their home.
The company owned everything.
Thurber was built on coal. Mining began in 1886.
Between 1888 and 1921 it was one of the biggest coal producers in Texas.
At its peak around 1920, eight to ten thousand people from a dozen nations lived here.
Italians, Poles, Mexicans, and many more.
The company built and owned the houses, schools, churches, saloons, the power plant.
After the mines unionized in 1903, Thurber became famous for one thing.
The first totally closed-shop town in the country.
No union, no work. No company, no home.
So what killed it? Oil.
As railroads switched their engines from coal to oil, demand collapsed.
Wage cuts triggered bitter strikes in 1926 and 1927.
Miners took assistance and left for coalfields in other states.
By the late 1930s Thurber was a virtual ghost town.
Today that single brick smokestack is nearly all that remains of a city of ten thousand.
Visible right off Interstate 20, if you know to look up.
Number three mined something that could kill you just by touching it.
Number three. Terlingua, on the edge of Big Bend. A town built on poison.
From about 1900 to 1950 this was a top American producer of mercury.
Quicksilver. A metal so toxic it slowly poisons anyone who works it.
It began with a Chicago businessman named Howard Perry.
He got remote Texas land as payment for a debt.
It turned out to sit on rich cinnabar, the ore mercury comes from.
In 1903 he opened the Chisos Mining Company.
It became the biggest mercury producer in the region, at times in the whole nation.
In the 1910s and 20s as many as two thousand people lived here.
A company store, a hotel, a school, even a dance pavilion.
Both World Wars drove demand sky-high. Mercury went into detonators and explosives.
Flasks of Terlingua quicksilver shipped around the globe.
But mercury mining is a brutal cycle of boom and bust.
After the war, foreign competition and low prices made it impossible.
When the Chisos Mine closed in 1945, it left something staggering behind.
Roughly twenty-three miles of tunnels carved beneath the town.
Terlingua emptied out, its adobe walls melting back into the desert.
Today a small art community lives among the bones of the place.
Twenty-three miles of poisoned tunnels still run beneath their feet.
Number two does not haunt the desert. It haunts the bottom of a lake.
Number two. Old Bluffton, a Hill Country town sitting underwater right now.
This town is not ruined. It is drowned.
It lies at the bottom of Lake Buchanan, and returns only when the water leaves.
Bluffton was a thriving community. Homes, a cotton gin, a bank, a hotel, a cemetery.
Then came the New Deal and the Buchanan Dam, built to electrify the Hill Country.
Families sold their land to the river authority and prepared to move.
When the dam was finished in 1937, officials thought the lake would fill in years.
It took months. Over twenty inches of rain poured down.
The rising lake swallowed Old Bluffton whole.
It drowned under what is usually about thirty feet of water.
Here is what makes Bluffton unforgettable. It comes back.
In severe droughts the lake drops and the dead town rises.
Tombstones, the cotton gin, the old bank, the hotel foundations break the surface.
In one major drought, archaeologists rushed in before the lake reclaimed it.
Then the rains returned, and Bluffton sank again.
Waiting, patiently, for the next drought to bring it back.
And that brings us to number one. The city Texas erased not once, but twice.
Number one. Indianola, once a great Texas port, now mostly underwater in its own bay.
A city of five thousand people. A gateway into Texas.
Wiped off the map by hurricanes twice in eleven years.
After the second time, the survivors simply gave up.
In the mid-1800s it was the busiest Texas port after Galveston.
German, Polish, and Czech immigrants first set foot in Texas right here.
By the 1870s, five thousand people called it home.
Then came September 1875. A monstrous hurricane slammed into the city.
It killed between a hundred fifty and three hundred people.
It nearly destroyed the entire town. But Texans are stubborn. They rebuilt.
Indianola came back.
Then on August 19th, 1886, just eleven years later, a second hurricane struck.
And this time the storm was followed by fire.
Wind, water, and fire finished what 1875 had started.
The survivors looked at their twice-destroyed city and walked away.
By 1887, Indianola was abandoned for good.
Then the bay itself crept inland and swallowed the ruins.
Today the old courthouse foundation lies under the shallow water of Matagorda Bay.
A granite statue stands at the water's edge, facing the sea.
The doorway through which thousands of families entered Texas.
Now a name on a marker, and a few stones beneath the waves. Erased twice.
That is number one.
Twelve towns. Twelve dreams the railroads, the storms, the mines, and the floods took back.
Texas has hundreds more of these places. Far more than twelve.
