Number twelve. Toyah, out in Reeves County, in the dry far west of the state.

[VISUAL: slow pan across the broken windows of the old school, dark inside.]

Toyah began in the 1880s as a trading post. Its name comes from a Native word meaning "flowing water," for the artesian springs that fed the area.

[VISUAL: a trickle of water over rock; then a steam locomotive illustration.]

[VISUAL: faded street scene of small wooden storefronts.]

Number eleven. Sherwood, in Irion County.

[VISUAL: wide shot revealing empty land all around the courthouse.]

Sherwood was laid out in 1886. When Irion County organized in 1889, Sherwood became the county seat, and the people built that courthouse to be proud of. By 1900 it was basically the only town in the county.

[VISUAL: illustration of a small bustling county-seat square.]

Then came the railroad — and this is the pattern you'll see again and again today. In 1910, the Kansas City, Mexico and Orient Railway laid its line across the county... and missed Sherwood.

### #9 — INDEPENDENCE — 5:30–7:10

Number nine. Independence, in Washington County, near Brenham.

[VISUAL: portrait-style illustration of Sam Houston.]

Independence was founded in 1835. By 1845 it was considered the wealthiest community in Texas, a Baptist stronghold.

[VISUAL: prosperous 1840s town illustration, fine homes.]

That same year, Baylor University was chartered — and built its very first campus right here, on Academy Hill. Sam Houston, the hero of Texas independence, gave one of the first major donations to start the school. He and his family lived in Independence in the 1850s, and in 1854 he was baptized in the nearby Brazos River.

[VISUAL: a river baptism scene, soft morning light.]

So how does a town this important become a ghost? Same villain as before. The railroads went elsewhere.

[VISUAL: a rail map with lines reaching neighboring towns and skipping Independence.]

[VISUAL: "BAYLOR → WACO" on the map; the columns left behind.]

The university left. The people followed. And those stone columns now stand in a quiet park — the ruins of the place where one of Texas's great institutions, and one of its greatest legends, once stood side by side.

### #8 — CALVERT — 7:10–8:55

[VISUAL: number "8" over a long block of grand but faded Victorian buildings.]

Number eight. Calvert, in Robertson County — and at its peak, this was the fourth-largest city in the entire state of Texas.

[VISUAL: sweeping shot down a street of ornate two-story 1800s storefronts.]

Let that land. Not the fourth-largest small town. The fourth-largest city in Texas. Around ten thousand people. An opera house. Saloons, gambling houses, and in 1871 a claim to the largest cotton gin in the world.

[VISUAL: enormous old cotton gin, bales stacked high.]

Calvert was born in 1868 when the Houston and Texas Central Railway arrived. Cotton was king, and Calvert was its court — a wealthy, diverse boomtown of merchants and workers from all over.

[VISUAL: busy 1870s cotton market, wagons full of bales.]

So what brought down the fourth-largest city in Texas? A beetle. The boll weevil — an insect that devours cotton — swept through, and combined with collapsing cotton prices, it gutted the local economy.

[VISUAL: extreme close-up of a boll weevil on a cotton boll; then a withered field.]

The decline was brutal. By 1900 the population had already fallen to around three thousand, and it kept dropping. In 1938, Calvert's great cotton gin and oil press shut down for good — the symbolic end of the dream.

[VISUAL: padlocked gin doors, "CLOSED."]

But here's the eerie part. Calvert never tore itself down. Its historic district is one of the largest in the country — dozens of full blocks of Victorian buildings, still standing, mostly empty. A fourth-largest city, frozen in time, brought to its knees by a bug.

[SFX: a fly buzzing, then silence.]

### #7 — SHAFTER — 8:55–10:30

[VISUAL: number "7" over crumbling adobe walls and a tall stone chimney in the Chinati Mountains.]

Number seven. Shafter, deep in the desert of Presidio County — once known as the Silver Capital of Texas.

[VISUAL: pieces of raw silver ore glinting; mine headframe silhouette.]

The hook: in just under sixty years, this single mining district pulled nearly thirty-one million ounces of silver out of the ground.

[VISUAL: stacks of silver bars, then dissolve to the empty desert.]

It started around 1880, when a man named John Spencer found silver in the Chinati Mountains. He partnered with a former Army colonel, William Shafter, and in 1883 founded the Presidio Mining Company. The town that grew up around the mine took the colonel's name.

[VISUAL: old company-town photo — rows of small adobe houses, a company store.]

At its height, around 1940, Shafter had as many as four thousand people. Company housing, a company store, a company doctor — your whole life owned by the mine.

[VISUAL: a single dim lightbulb in a miner's adobe room.]

But silver mining lives and dies on price. Operations stalled when silver crashed in the 1930s. Then rising costs, a shortage of miners, and a unionization fight pushed the American Metal Company to shut the operation down in the 1940s.

[VISUAL: mine gates chained shut, ore cart rusting.]

When the nearby military fields closed after World War Two, Shafter's decline turned into a free fall. From four thousand people... down to a couple dozen. The Silver Capital of Texas, returned to the desert that hid the silver in the first place.

[VISUAL: number "6" over a faded motel sign and an empty stretch of old cracked highway.]

Number six. Glenrio — a town that sits exactly on the Texas–New Mexico line.

[VISUAL: a state-line marker, one foot in each state, painted on the road.]

The hook: this is one of the best-preserved ghost towns from the golden age of Route 66 — frozen at the moment the road died.

[VISUAL: classic neon-era motel, "MOTEL" sign, diner, gas station — all empty.]

Glenrio was born around 1903 when the railroad came through. But its real life began in 1926, when the legendary Route 66 — Chicago to Santa Monica — was routed straight through town.

[VISUAL: vintage Route 66 shield; a 1950s car cruising past cafes.]

Suddenly Glenrio was a highway oasis. Motels, gas stations, the Texas Longhorn Café — all feeding off the endless river of travelers.

[VISUAL: busy mid-century roadside, families gassing up.]

And the border made it wonderfully strange. The Texas side had no bars, because that county was dry. The New Mexico side had no gas stations, because New Mexico's fuel tax was higher. The post office sat on one side; the mail arrived on the other.

[VISUAL: split-screen graphic — "TX: no bars / NM: no gas."]

Then came the killer that ended a thousand small towns: the interstate. By 1973, Interstate 40 was finished, and it bypassed Glenrio completely.

[VISUAL: cars streaming past on I-40 in the distance while the old strip sits silent.]

The travelers vanished overnight. Today the motel, the café, the Texaco station, and the old roadbed all still stand — a perfectly preserved snapshot of the day the Mother Road went quiet.

[SFX: a lone car passing fast on a distant freeway.]

[VISUAL: number "5" over a weathered wooden building and an old jail, dusk light.]

Number five. Helena, in Karnes County — once self-proclaimed "the toughest town on earth."

Helena was founded in 1852 and became the first county seat of Karnes County — a rough stop between San Antonio and the coast, with saloons, hotels, a courthouse, and a violent reputation.

[VISUAL: rowdy frontier saloon illustration.]

How violent? Helena gave its name to the "Helena Duel" — where two men had their left hands tied together, each handed a knife with a short three-inch blade, and were left to fight. [VERIFY: "Helena Duel" is documented in local histories but is partly legend — frame as such.]

[VISUAL: a hand signing a railroad right-of-way document.]

And he had the power to do it. He offered free right-of-way to the railroad — on the condition that the tracks be routed away from Helena, through a new town instead. The railroad took the deal.

That detour, still called "The Big Curve," strangled Helena. Business followed the trains. In 1894 the county seat moved to Karnes City. And the toughest town on earth slowly died — they say, exactly as a grieving father intended.

### #4 — THURBER — 14:00–16:00

[VISUAL: number "4" over a single tall brick smokestack standing alone beside the interstate.]

Number four. Thurber, in Erath County — and at its peak it was the largest company town in Texas.

[VISUAL: that lone smokestack against the sky; cars on I-20 below it.]

The hook: ten thousand people once lived here, and not one of them owned their home. The company owned everything.

[VISUAL: rows of identical company houses; a "TEXAS & PACIFIC COAL CO." sign.]

Thurber was built on coal. Mining began in 1886, and the Texas and Pacific Coal Company ran the whole show. Between 1888 and 1921, this was one of the largest coal producers in the state.

[VISUAL: miners descending into a dark shaft, lanterns glowing.]

At its peak around 1920, Thurber had eight to ten thousand people, from more than a dozen nationalities — Italians, Poles, Mexicans, and many more. The company built and owned the houses, the schools, the churches, the saloons, the stores, the electric plant. Everything.

[VISUAL: a bustling company main street; a paycheck stamped with the company name.]

It was so tightly controlled that after the mines unionized in 1903, Thurber became known as the first totally closed-shop town in the country — you couldn't work there without the union, and you couldn't live there without the company.

[VISUAL: union banner over a crowd of miners.]

So what killed it? Oil. As railroads switched their locomotives from coal to oil, demand for Thurber's coal collapsed. Wage cuts triggered bitter strikes in 1926 and 1927. Miners took assistance and left for coal fields in other states.

[VISUAL: families loading wagons, leaving town.]

By the late 1930s, Thurber was a virtual ghost town. Today, that single brick smokestack and a couple of old buildings are nearly all that remain of a city of ten thousand — visible right off Interstate 20, if you know to look up.

[SFX: a long, low industrial groan fading out.]

Number three is a mining ghost town too — but this one mined something that could kill you just by touching it.

### #3 — TERLINGUA — 16:00–18:15

[VISUAL: number "3" over roofless adobe ruins scattered across a hillside near the mountains of Big Bend.]

Number three. Terlingua, on the edge of Big Bend — a ghost town built on poison.

[VISUAL: blood-red cinnabar ore in rock; eerie close-up.]

The hook: from about 1900 to 1950, this remote corner of Texas was one of the top producers in America of mercury — quicksilver — a metal so toxic it slowly poisons anyone who works it.

[VISUAL: silvery liquid mercury beading and rolling; flasks lined up.]

It began with a Chicago businessman named Howard Perry. He'd received some remote Texas land as payment for a debt — and it turned out to sit on top of rich cinnabar deposits, the ore mercury comes from. In 1903 he opened the Chisos Mining Company.

[VISUAL: portrait-style figure over a map of Big Bend; mine headframe.]

The Chisos Mine became the biggest mercury producer in the region — at times in the entire nation. In the boom of the 1910s and 20s, as many as two thousand people lived in Terlingua, with a company store, a hotel, a school, even a dance pavilion.

[VISUAL: lively company-town scene, the dance pavilion lit up.]

World Wars One and Two drove demand sky-high — mercury went into detonators and explosives. Flasks of Terlingua quicksilver were shipped around the globe.

[VISUAL: crates marked for export; a steam train pulling away.]

But mercury mining is a brutal cycle of boom and bust. After World War Two, foreign competition and low prices made it impossible. When the Chisos Mine finally closed in 1945, it left behind something staggering: roughly twenty-three miles of tunnels carved into the rock beneath the town.

[VISUAL: dark mine tunnel stretching into blackness.]

Terlingua emptied out — its adobe buildings melting back into the desert. Today it's a hauntingly beautiful ruin near Big Bend, with a small art community living among the bones. Twenty-three miles of poisoned tunnels still run beneath their feet.

[SFX/MUSIC: tense, shimmering note.]

Number two doesn't haunt the desert. It haunts the bottom of a lake.

### #2 — BLUFFTON — 18:15–20:00

[VISUAL: number "2" over a calm lake surface; then drop beneath the water to murky foundations.]

Number two. Old Bluffton — a Hill Country town that is, right now, sitting underwater.

[VISUAL: aerial of Lake Buchanan, vast and blue.]

The hook: this town isn't ruined. It's drowned. It lies at the bottom of Lake Buchanan, and it only comes back when the water disappears.

[VISUAL: a tombstone breaking the surface of receding water.]

Bluffton was a real, thriving Hill Country community — homes, a cotton gin, a bank, a hotel, a cemetery. Then came the New Deal, and the Buchanan Dam, built to bring electricity to the Hill Country.

[VISUAL: 1930s dam construction, concrete rising; the Colorado River below.]

Families sold their land to the river authority and slowly prepared to move as the waters were expected to rise. When the dam was completed in 1937, officials thought it would take years for the lake to fill.

[VISUAL: a calendar flipping; water creeping up across the land.]

It took months. More than twenty inches of rain poured down, and the rising lake swallowed Old Bluffton whole — drowning the town under what is usually about thirty feet of water.

[VISUAL: water closing over rooftops; bubbles; then stillness.]

And here's what makes Bluffton unforgettable. It comes back. During severe droughts the lake drops and the dead town rises — tombstones, the cotton gin's remains, the old bank, the hotel foundations, breaking the surface like a memory.

[VISUAL: people walking among exposed ruins on cracked lakebed, 2009-style drought scene.]

In one major drought, the water fell so far that archaeologists rushed in to excavate the town before the lake reclaimed it. Then the rains returned, and Bluffton sank again — waiting, patiently, for the next drought to bring it back. [VERIFY: confirm most recent re-emergence year before publish.]

### #1 — INDIANOLA — 20:00–22:30

[VISUAL: number "1" slams on screen over a violent illustration of a hurricane hitting a 1800s port town.]

Number one. Indianola — once a great Texas port, now mostly underwater in the bay where it stood.

[VISUAL: a grand 1870s port — ships, warehouses, a busy waterfront main street.]

The hook I promised you at the start: this was a city of five thousand people, a gateway to Texas — and it was wiped off the map by hurricanes twice in eleven years. After the second time, they simply gave up.

[VISUAL: immigrant ship arriving; families with trunks coming down the gangplank.]

In the mid-1800s, Indianola was one of the busiest ports on the Texas coast — second only to Galveston. This was where waves of German, Polish, and Czech immigrants first set foot in Texas, arriving by steamship. By the 1870s, five thousand people called it home.

[VISUAL: bustling diverse waterfront, multiple languages on shop signs.]

Then came September 1875. A monstrous hurricane slammed into Indianola. The storm killed somewhere between a hundred and fifty and three hundred people and nearly destroyed the entire city.

[VISUAL: 1875 storm — water surging through streets, buildings collapsing.]

But Texans are stubborn. They rebuilt. Indianola came back.

[VISUAL: reconstruction, new buildings rising on the same waterfront.]

And then, on August 19th, 1886 — just eleven years later — a second, even more intense hurricane struck the rebuilt town. And this time the storm was followed by fire.

[VISUAL: storm and flames together — the unthinkable, water and fire destroying the same streets.]

That was the end. Wind, water, and fire finished what 1875 had started. The survivors looked at the wreckage of their twice-destroyed city... and walked away. By 1887, Indianola was abandoned for good.

[VISUAL: empty, debris-strewn shoreline, a lone chimney standing.]

But the most haunting part is what happened next. The bay itself crept inland and swallowed the ruins. Today, the foundation of the old courthouse and pieces of the main street lie under the shallow water of Matagorda Bay.

[VISUAL: clear shallow water over a stone foundation; a granite memorial statue standing at the water's edge.]

A great Texas city — the doorway through which thousands of families entered this state — is now a name on a marker, a statue facing the sea, and a few stones beneath the waves. Erased not once, but twice. That is number one.
